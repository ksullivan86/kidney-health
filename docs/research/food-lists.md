# Food lists for CKD stage 3b–5 with type 1 diabetes

Research note behind `docs/diet-guide.md`. Written 2026-10-05. Nutrient values are USDA FoodData Central **SR Legacy** figures (April 2018 release, downloaded as CSV and computed per serving with USDA household-measure gram weights) and are marked *approx.* throughout; brands vary, so the food log should prefer the label when one exists. Reference numbers in brackets point to the source list at the end.

> **Read this first.** These lists are a reading guide for conversations with the person's **nephrologist and renal dietitian**, not a replacement for them. Every guideline cited here (KDOQI 2020, KDIGO 2024, ADA 2026) says the same thing: potassium, phosphorus, sodium, protein and fluid targets are set from the person's *own* labs and medicines, and a renal dietitian should individualise them [1][2][3]. A food that is "avoid" below can be fine for someone whose serum potassium is normal, and a food that is "eat" can be wrong for someone on a fluid restriction. Nothing here changes insulin dosing; that is the diabetes team's decision.

**Who this is written for:** one adult with CKD **stage G3b–G5 (eGFR < 45), not on dialysis unless a line says otherwise**, who also has **type 1 diabetes** and doses insulin against carbohydrate. Where dialysis changes the advice it is called out in a *Dialysis* note.

**Sorting rules used for the three lists** (per usual serving):

* Potassium: American Kidney Fund cut-offs **≤ 150 mg low, 151–250 medium, ≥ 251 high** [12]; the National Kidney Foundation calls ≥ 200 mg "high" [8][15].
* Phosphorus: AKF **≤ 100 mg low, 101–199 medium, ≥ 200 high** [13]; *any* "phos" ingredient on the label counts as high regardless of the number [7][9].
* Sodium: FDA **≤ 140 mg = "low sodium"**, ≤ 5 % DV (≤ 115 mg) low, **≥ 20 % DV (≥ 460 mg) high** [16]; NKF's shopping rule is ≤ 240 mg per serving and "sodium (mg) less than calories" [9].
* **List A** = low in all three (K ≤ 150, P ≤ 100, Na ≤ 200) at the stated serving, or the standard renal substitute for a problem food (marked †). Protein foods are listed in A only when a 3-oz portion stays under ~220 mg K and ~200 mg P.
* **List B** = medium in one nutrient, or fine only at the stated portion.
* **List C** = high in one nutrient at a normal serving, carries a phosphate/potassium additive, is a known toxin (star fruit), or is a sugary drink that a person with T1D should not drink except to treat a low.
* In the tables, **bold** marks a value that crosses a "high" threshold (K ≥ 251, P ≥ 200, Na ≥ 460).

**How stage and dialysis change the lists.** The guideline targets are written for "CKD 3–5 not on dialysis" as one block, so lists A–C do not change between 3b, 4 and 5; what changes is *how likely* a restriction is to be needed (hyperkalaemia prevalence in people with diabetes rises from ~9 % at G3/A1 to ~34 % at G5/A3 [2]), so a person at G3b with normal potassium may be told to use List B freely, while at G5 the same person may be told to treat List B as "once a day". On **haemodialysis** protein portions go up to 4–6 oz per meal (1.0–1.2 g/kg), potassium stays restricted, phosphorus binders are taken with meals and fluid is limited; on **peritoneal dialysis** potassium is often liberalised and the dialysate glucose must be counted as carbohydrate [1][4][23].

## A. EAT / CHOOSE (lower potassium, lower phosphorus, lower sodium, sensible carbohydrate)

Portions are deliberately small: ½ cup fruit or vegetable, 3 oz cooked meat/fish (NIDDK's renal-diet portion), because *a large serving of a low-potassium food becomes a high-potassium food* [5][8]. "Hypo treatment?" marks what works for the ADA 15-g rule: ★ = good (≈ 15 g fast carbohydrate, little potassium or phosphorus); ✗ = too little or too slow (fibre/fat) to rely on [3][6].

> **Safety first.** The ★ ranking is for choosing *between* options. A low (< 70 mg/dL) is always treated immediately with 15 g of fast carbohydrate from whatever is at hand—orange juice, regular cola or milk included—then rechecked at 15 minutes and repeated if still low. Never take less than 15 g, and never use "sugar-free" sweets, because of potassium or phosphorus; one rescue portion of orange juice (~250 mg K) is a logging note, not a danger. Level 3 hypoglycaemia (needing another person's help) means glucagon and emergency services [3]. The app should log hypo treatments against the potassium budget but never warn against or block them.

## B. LIMIT / PORTION CAREFULLY

These are medium-potassium (151–250 mg), medium-phosphorus (101–199 mg) or moderately salty foods, or foods that are low only at a small portion. The usual dietitian rule is **no more than one or two List B servings per meal, at the serving shown**, with fruit/vegetable choices from List A filling the rest. > **Leaching potassium (NKF method).** Peel, slice ⅛-inch thick, rinse in warm water, soak at least 2 hours in warm unsalted water (10 parts water to 1 part vegetable; change water every 4 hours if soaking longer), rinse again, then cook in 5 parts fresh unsalted water. KDIGO 2024 cites a halving of potassium with 5–10 minutes' soaking in previously boiled water [2]. Bethke & Jansky (J Food Sci 2008) found that soaking alone removed no potassium, boiling 1-cm cubes removed ~50 % and boiling shredded potato ~75 %, and that leaching before boiling added nothing—so double-boiling small pieces (boil, drain, boil again in fresh water) performs about as well as the long soak [23]. ## D. Label-reading checklist (print this)

Phosphorus is **not required** on the US Nutrition Facts panel; potassium **is** now required (FDA 2016 label rule), and the Daily Values on the panel are for healthy adults—sodium 2,300 mg, potassium 4,700 mg, phosphorus 1,250 mg—so %DV for potassium and phosphorus are misleading for CKD; read the milligrams [9][16].

1. **Ingredient list first: anything containing "PHOS".** Phosphoric acid, mono/di/trisodium phosphate, sodium hexametaphosphate, sodium tripolyphosphate, tetrasodium pyrophosphate, sodium acid pyrophosphate, di/tricalcium phosphate, monocalcium phosphate, potassium phosphate/dipotassium phosphate, aluminium phosphate, "pyrophosphate", "polyphosphate" [5][7]. Any hit = treat as high-phosphorus whatever the number says. Typical carriers: dark colas and pepper sodas, bottled coffee/tea drinks, processed and spreadable cheese, non-dairy creamers, instant puddings and cake/pancake/biscuit mixes, "enhanced" fresh meat and poultry, deli meats and hot dogs, frozen entrées and pizza, fast food, some "fortified" cereals and plant milks.
2. **Potassium additives.** "Potassium chloride" (salt substitutes, many "reduced-sodium" soups, broths, sauces, deli meats, canned vegetables), potassium lactate (processed meat), potassium citrate/phosphate/sorbate, acesulfame-K sweetener. Additive potassium is ~90 % absorbed—worse than the same milligrams from fruit [2].
3. **Potassium milligrams.** Low ≤ 150 mg, medium 151–250, high ≥ 251 per *your* serving (AKF) [12]; NKF's shopping cue is ≤ 200 mg [8]. If the label says "0 mg" check that the serving is not absurdly small.
4. **Sodium.** %DV: **5 % or less (≤ 115 mg) is low, 20 % or more (≥ 460 mg) is high** [5][16]. FDA words: "sodium-free" < 5 mg, "very low" ≤ 35 mg, "low sodium" ≤ 140 mg, "reduced" = 25 % less than the original (which can still be a lot); "unsalted/no salt added" is about added salt only [16]. NKF rule of thumb: ≤ 240 mg per serving and milligrams of sodium less than calories [9]. Watch for sodium in sweet things (cereal, bread, cake mixes) and in "healthy" items (cottage cheese, canned beans, tomato products).
5. **Carbohydrate (diabetes).** Use **Total Carbohydrate** per serving, then multiply by how many servings you actually eat. Fibre: foods with ≥ 5 g fibre per serving slow the rise; subtract fibre only if your diabetes team has taught you to. Sugar alcohols: count roughly half the grams (erythritol ~0) and remember a "sugar-free" candy will **not** treat a low [23]. "Added sugars" line: minimise outside hypo treatments. Milk alternatives: unsweetened almond 3 g vs rice milk 22 g vs oat milk 16–24 g per cup.
6. **Protein and the P:protein ratio.** Phosphorus (if listed) ÷ protein grams: < 10–12 mg per gram is efficient protein (egg white, chicken breast, fresh fish); > 15 (processed cheese, nuts, beans, colas with zero protein) is not [23].
7. **Serving size vs what you eat.** A 20-oz soda is 2.5 servings; a 'small' bag of chips is often 2; cereal cups are usually 1½ of the stated serving—multiply everything.
8. **Fluid (if restricted).** Soups, gelatin, ice cream, ice pops and gravy all count as fluid [10].

## E. Restaurant ordering tips

From NKF *Dining Out with Confidence* [10] and AKF's fast-casual guidance [14]:

* **Plan the day around the meal.** Eat lower-sodium, lower-potassium at the other meals; "moderation is key". Look up the menu and nutrition information online beforehand; restaurants that cook to order are easiest. If fluid-restricted, bank fluid for the evening.
* **Standard requests that work:** "dressing / gravy / sauce on the side"; "no salt, please" on grilled, sautéed or baked entrées; "no MSG, no soy or fish sauce" in Asian restaurants; "no cheese", "catsup on the side", "no tomatoes" on burgers and sandwiches; green beans or rice instead of a baked potato or fries.
* **Protein portion:** restaurant lunch portions are 4–6 oz and dinner 6–8 oz—two to three times the 3-oz renal portion. Order half portions, share a main, or box half before you start. *Dialysis:* the larger portion may be what you need; ask the dietitian.
* **Better choices:** grilled or baked chicken, fish, steak or burger without cheese; fajitas or tacos (no cheese, no beans); omelettes with low-K vegetables (mushroom, squash, peppers) and sauce on the side; steamed rice, plain noodles or pasta, unsalted bread, lettuce salad with oil and vinegar or lemon; fruit cup, canned peaches/pears/pineapple; angel food, sugar cookies, vanilla wafers, sherbet or fruit ice; non-cola soda, lemonade, iced tea, water with lemon.
* **Poor choices:** anything cured or smoked (ham, bacon, sausage, lox, corned beef, prosciutto, chorizo); soups, bouillon, consommé; cheese dishes, cream sauces, alfredo, nachos, potato skins, quiche; tomato, cheese or steak/Worcestershire/soy/hot sauces; baked/fried potatoes, sweet potato, fried rice, beans and refried beans, stewed tomatoes, spinach, collards, artichoke, acorn squash; salads with spinach, tomato, avocado, kidney/garbanzo beans, nuts, seeds, olives, pickles; melon, oranges, bananas, kiwi, dried fruit, **star fruit**; colas, milk shakes, cocoa, orange/tomato/vegetable juice, fruit-juice cocktails, beer; chocolate, coconut, nut, cream-cheese or pumpkin desserts.
* **Cuisines:** Chinese—steamed rice not fried, stir-fried low-K vegetables (snow peas, string beans, water chestnuts, bean sprouts, bok choy), no sauce poured on, skip the soup; French—cream and butter sauces mean phosphorus, pommes frites mean potassium, French bread is fine; Mexican—order à la carte, skip beans and heavy cheese; Italian—pasta with oil/garlic or pesto rather than tomato, cheese or alfredo sauce; fast food—grilled items, kid's size or à la carte, no cheese, no sauces, side salad with vinaigrette, water or non-cola soda [10][14].
* **Diabetes specifics:** know the carbohydrate of the plate before you bolus—restaurant rice/pasta portions are often 90 g carb; sauces add sugar; alcohol only with food and with a plan for delayed lows [23]; carry glucose tablets so the hypo treatment is never a cola or orange juice.

## G. Notes on the numbers and how they were checked

* Source database: USDA FoodData Central **SR Legacy** (release April 2018, the final Standard Reference), downloaded as the full CSV from https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/download-datasets and queried locally; nutrient IDs 1092 (K), 1091 (P), 1093 (Na), 1005 (carbohydrate by difference), 1003 (protein). Serving gram weights are USDA household measures from `food_portion.csv` (e.g., 1 cup blueberries = 148 g, 1 medium banana = 118 g); where USDA had no matching measure a standard weight was used (3 oz = 85 g; 1 tbsp oil = 13.5 g). The same values are available per food at `https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/<FDC ID>/nutrients` and via the API (`https://api.nal.usda.gov/fdc/v1/food/<FDC ID>?api_key=DEMO_KEY`, ~30 requests/hour on the demo key). FDC IDs for every row are in Appendix 1 so the app's `scripts/curated_foods.py` can reuse them.
* Cross-checks: the AKF Kidney Kitchen potassium and phosphorus guides [12][13] agree with the USDA figures used here to within rounding for the foods both list (e.g., banana 422, avocado ½ 488, spinach cooked ½ cup 420, cantaloupe cup 417, honeydew cup 388, prune juice ½ cup 354, almonds 198 vs 208 here, cod 207, shrimp 220, beef liver 422 mg P, All-Bran 632 mg K). NKF's phosphorus page [7] and dining guide [10] were used for the qualitative "high/low" classifications.
* USDA under-counts additive phosphorus (it reports what the sample contained, often from older analyses, and many products reformulate); for colas the measured literature range (40–62 mg per 12 oz) is quoted instead [19]. For any food with a "phos" ingredient treat the mg figure as a floor.
* Potassium from whole plant foods is ~50–60 % absorbed, from animal foods 70–90 %, from additives/salt substitutes ~90 % (KDIGO 2024, Figure 33) [2]; the tables report *total* potassium, which is how every renal handout and the Nutrition Facts panel report it.
* Two kidney.org pages (potassium, sodium) returned HTTP 403/404 to automated fetching during this review; their content was confirmed through NKF's own PDFs [9][10], the NKF text as indexed in search results [8], and the UF/IFAS extension summary of the same leaching method [22].
* The ADA 2026 Section 11 full text is at PMC12690176 (verified 2026-10-05): Rec 11.3 protein 0.8 g/kg/day (A) and 1.0–1.2 g/kg/day on dialysis (B) — the same wording the 2025 edition numbered 11.8 — and Rec 11.5 BP < 130/80 mmHg (systolic < 120 where safe) [4][23].
* Fact-check 2026-10-05: all 263 table rows in Sections A–C were recomputed from the SR Legacy CSV using the Appendix 1 gram weights and matched within rounding (K/P/Na within 4 mg or 5 %; carbohydrate and protein within 0.6 g or 10 %). See `docs/research/fact-check.md`.
