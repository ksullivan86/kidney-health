# Kidney-friendly eating with type 1 diabetes: a guide for family and caregivers

*Written 2026-10-05 from the fact-checked research notes in `docs/research/` (`ckd-diet.md`, `t1d-and-ckd.md`, `food-lists.md`, `fact-check.md`). Numbers in brackets point to the Sources list at the end. Nutrient values are USDA FoodData Central SR Legacy figures per stated serving and are approximate; brands vary, so when a food has a label, the label wins.*

> **Please read this first.**
> This guide and the kidney-health app **support** the person's care team; they never **replace** it. The nephrologist, the diabetes team and the renal dietitian set the real targets for potassium, phosphorus, sodium, protein, fluid and blood glucose from the person's own blood tests, urine output, medicines and insulin plan, and they change them as the labs change. Every guideline cited here says this explicitly: KDIGO 2024 asks clinicians to "use renal dietitians or accredited nutrition providers" to tailor sodium, phosphorus, potassium and protein "to their individual needs" (Practice Point 3.3.2) [2]. The numbers below are *starting points* that the care team should overwrite in the app. **Nothing here changes an insulin dose.** A food marked "avoid" can be fine for someone whose potassium is normal; a food marked "eat" can be wrong for someone on a fluid limit.

---

## 1. The short version

Kidneys that are failing lose the ability to get rid of **potassium**, **phosphorus**, **sodium**, the waste from **protein**, and (late on) **water**. The diet exists to keep those five in a range the kidneys can still handle, while the sixth number, **carbohydrate**, keeps doing exactly what it always did in type 1 diabetes: it decides the insulin dose.

In practice this means:

* **Portions, not bans.** Most "kidney diet" problems are size problems. Half a cup of fruit or vegetables, 3 oz of meat (a deck of cards), 1 oz of cheese. "A large serving of a low-potassium food becomes a high-potassium food" (NKF) [5].
* **Fresh over packaged.** More than 70 % of sodium comes from packaged and restaurant food, not the salt shaker [34], and the phosphate *additives* in processed food are absorbed almost completely while natural phosphorus is not [12][13].
* **Read the ingredient list for "PHOS" and "potassium chloride".** Phosphorus is not required on the Nutrition Facts panel [20][38].
* **A low blood sugar is always treated first.** Use glucose tablets or apple/cranberry juice when there is a choice, but if orange juice or cola is what is within reach, drink the measured 4 oz now, recheck in 15 minutes and log it. One rescue portion is a budgeting note; an untreated low is an emergency [8][9].
* **Star fruit is never allowed** at any stage of kidney disease [39][17].
* **Protein goes *up*, not down, if dialysis starts** (from 0.6–0.8 to 1.0–1.2 g per kg), and fluid becomes limited [1][3].

## 2. The five numbers to watch, and the sixth

| Number | What it does | Why it is limited in CKD | What happens when it is not |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Potassium (K)** | Keeps nerves and the heart's rhythm working. About 90 % of dietary potassium leaves through the kidney. | As GFR falls, blood potassium rises. Insulin shortage, high glucose, ACE inhibitors/ARBs, finerenone, constipation and acidosis all push it higher [2][36]. In people with diabetes, hyperkalaemia prevalence rises from 8.8 % at G3/A1 to 34.4 % at G5/A3 [2]. | **Hyperkalaemia** (K > 5.0–5.5 mmol/L): heart-rhythm disturbance and sudden cardiac death; K ≥ 6.5 mmol/L is an emergency [2]. |
| **Phosphorus (P)** | Builds bone with calcium. | Filtered by the kidney; retention starts at stage 3–4 and dialysis removes it only partly [1][10]. | **Hyperphosphataemia** (normal 2.5–4.5 mg/dL [20]): calcium is pulled out of bone and deposited in "blood vessels, lungs, eyes, and heart" (NKF) [20]; higher phosphorus intake is tied to higher mortality on dialysis (HR 2.37 highest vs lowest tertile) [14]. |
| **Sodium (Na)** | Holds water in the body. | Retained sodium means retained water. | High blood pressure, swelling, protein in urine, thirst that breaks fluid limits on dialysis [1][2]. |
| **Protein** | Builds and repairs muscle; its waste (urea) is cleared by the kidney. | Protein waste and the extra filtering work speed up kidney damage. Too *little* protein causes wasting, which is the bigger danger on dialysis and in diabetes [1][2][3]. | > 1.3 g/kg/day: faster eGFR loss, albuminuria, cardiovascular death [2][8]. < 0.6 g/kg: malnutrition and hypoglycaemia risk [1][3]. |
| **Fluid** | — | Only once urine output falls (late stage 5 / dialysis) does water have nowhere to go. | Swelling, breathlessness, pulmonary oedema, heart failure; weight gain between dialysis sessions > 3.5 % of body weight is linked to death [28]. |
| **Carbohydrate (the sixth)** | The number insulin is dosed against. | Not a kidney target at all. Renal swaps (white bread, white rice, canned fruit) are lower in fibre and raise glucose faster, so the insulin-to-carb ratio may need re-tuning with the diabetes team [3][16]. | Highs and lows. High glucose itself pulls potassium out of cells [36]. |

### Typical daily targets by stage and dialysis status

The guidelines set *one* range for "CKD 3–5 not on dialysis" and a second for dialysis; what changes with stage is how likely a restriction is to be *triggered* by the labs [1][2]. Potassium and phosphorus have **no guideline mg number**: KDOQI says to adjust intake "to maintain serum potassium within the normal range" (6.4.1) and to keep serum phosphate normal (6.3.1) [1]; the figures below are the ranges renal dietitians use once a restriction is ordered.

For a 70 kg adult: protein 42–56 g/day before dialysis, 70–84 g/day on dialysis; energy 1,750–2,450 kcal [1][2]. The app's exact starting values per stage are in section 7.

### Day by day, or weekly average?

Not every number is judged the same way. **Potassium, sodium and fluid act within the day.** Blood potassium can move within hours of a large load, and a low day does not "bank" against a high one; the goal is to never have a dangerous day [2]. On **hemodialysis**, the three accumulate until the next session, so they are also judged over the gap *between* sessions: the long weekend gap is the dangerous one, with all-cause mortality on the day after it 22.1 vs 18.0 per 100 patient-years and hospitalisations for rhythm disturbance roughly doubled [53], and interdialytic weight gain above 3.5 % of body weight independently predicts death [28]. **Phosphorus and protein are judged on the average over a week or so.** Serum phosphate, PTH and nutritional status reflect weeks of intake, not one meal; KDOQI 2020 itself assesses what someone eats from multi-day food records rather than a single day [1][13], and one high-phosphorus dinner is simply balanced by lower days around it. **Carbohydrate** is counted per meal and per day for insulin; its weekly average is informational. The app follows exactly this split (section 7).

## 3. What to eat, limit and avoid

Sorting rules used (per usual serving): potassium low ≤ 150 mg, medium 151–250, high ≥ 251 (AKF) [46]; NKF calls ≥ 200 mg "high" [5]. Phosphorus low ≤ 100, medium 101–199, high ≥ 200, and *any* "phos" ingredient counts as high [46][20]. Sodium: FDA "low" ≤ 140 mg; ≥ 460 mg (20 % DV) is high [34]. **Bold** marks a value over a "high" line. All values approximate (USDA SR Legacy) [51].

## 4. Type 1 diabetes specifics

### Carb counting with renal swaps

Nothing about CKD changes carb counting: rapid-acting insulin is still dosed against grams of carbohydrate (ADA 2026 Rec 5.27), and people on fixed doses keep consistent carbohydrate "with respect to time and amount" (Rec 5.28) [8]. NKF's CKD carb-counting page uses the same arithmetic, 15 g = 1 serving, 3–6 servings per meal and 1–3 per snack, adjusted by the dietitian [7].

The collision is that renal swaps move carbohydrate toward refined foods: white rice for brown, white bread for whole-wheat, Cream of Wheat for oatmeal, canned fruit for a banana. That lowers potassium and phosphorus but cuts fibre and raises glycaemic load, so **expect the insulin-to-carb ratio to need re-tuning with the diabetes team** [3][16]. Per serving (USDA) [51]:

| Swap | Carb g | Fibre g | K mg | P mg | What it costs or saves |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| White rice 1 cup → brown rice 1 cup | 45 → 52 | 0.6 → 3.2 | 55 → 174 | 68 → 208 | brown costs ~120 mg K, ~140 mg P |
| White bread 1 slice → whole-wheat | 14 → 14 | 0.8 → 1.9 | 37 → 81 | 28 → 68 | +45 mg K, +40 mg P per slice |
| Cream of Wheat 1 cup → oatmeal 1 cup | 26 → 28 | 1.3 → 4.0 | 40 → 164 | 38 → 180 | oatmeal costs ~125 mg K, ~140 mg P |
| Banana small → blueberries ½ cup | 23 → 11 | 2.6 → 1.8 | 362 → 57 | 22 → 9 | saves 305 mg K and half the carb |
| Potato baked → white rice ½ cup | 37 → 22 | — | 926 → 28 | 121 → 34 | saves ~900 mg K |

Three practical rules: **(1)** whole grains are not automatically off the table: their phosphorus is phytate-bound and only ~20–50 % absorbed (vs ~40–60 % from meat and ~90–100 % from additives), and KDIGO 2022 explicitly recommends a diet "high in vegetables, fruits, whole grains, fiber, legumes" for diabetes with CKD (PP 3.1.1) [3][12][13]; if serum potassium and phosphate are in range, one or two whole-grain servings a day is a fair trade for fibre and flatter glucose, decided with the dietitian. **(2)** Portion the starch, do not just swap it: ⅔ cup white rice is 30 g and keeps the count where the ratio expects it. **(3)** Get fibre from low-potassium produce (berries, apples, green beans, cauliflower, cabbage, cucumber, peppers, zucchini, onions) [22]; berries and applesauce are the best value, low potassium *and* low carbohydrate per cup.

### Treating a low on a kidney diet

**The rule of 15 does not change.** Glucose < 70 mg/dL in a conscious person: take 15 g of fast carbohydrate (5–10 g if on an automated insulin delivery system), recheck in 15 minutes, repeat if still low, and avoid high-fat or high-protein foods for the initial treatment (ADA 2026 Rec 6.15, B). Level 1 is < 70 mg/dL, level 2 < 54 mg/dL, level 3 is any event needing another person's help; level 3 is treated with **glucagon** and emergency services, and glucagon (preferably a nasal or ready-to-use form) should be prescribed to everyone on insulin (Rec 6.16, A) [9]. If the next meal is more than an hour away, follow with a snack [11].

**Potassium never delays treatment.** The ranking below is about *which* 15 g to reach for when there is a choice. One 4-oz orange juice is about 250 mg potassium, which a day's budget absorbs; an untreated level 2–3 low is a medical emergency. Never under-dose, and never use "sugar-free" sweets (sugar alcohols) to treat a low [9][11].

### For the clinician: insulin and declining kidney function

Background only; dose changes are the prescriber's. The kidney clears a large share of injected insulin and performs about 20 % of gluconeogenesis; both fall with eGFR. The ADA/KDIGO consensus states insulin doses "may need to be decreased … due to reduced insulin clearance" and calls advanced CKD "a risk factor for hypoglycemia" [54]. Pecoits-Filho et al. cite older guidance of roughly a 25 % total-dose reduction at GFR 10–50 mL/min and 50 % below 10, report glargine/detemir requirements ~27–30 % lower at GFR < 60 while degludec needed no adjustment, and note that a restricted renal diet itself "reduces hepatic gluconeogenesis" [55]. Cardiovascular autonomic neuropathy (present in about two-thirds of people with advanced CKD in that series) blunts counter-regulation; ADA 2026 asks for at least annual screening for impaired hypoglycaemia awareness (Rec 6.11) [9][55]. On the potassium side: insulin deficiency and hyperglycaemia both raise serum potassium, diabetic nephropathy is the commonest cause of hyporeninaemic hypoaldosteronism, and ACE inhibitors, ARBs and finerenone all raise potassium, so a run of high readings, a missed basal dose or a new RAAS/MRA prescription are reasons to be stricter about dietary potassium that week and to expect a lab check [3][36].

### CGM and A1c

HbA1c becomes unreliable in G4–G5 and has "low reliability" on dialysis (KDIGO 2022 PP 2.1.2): anaemia, shortened red-cell life, ESA/iron therapy and transfusion push it **down**, carbamylated haemoglobin from uraemia pushes it **up** [3][55]. ADA 2026 says A1c "is less reliable at advanced CKD stages" and that glycated albumin and fructosamine (15–30-day averages) help [9]. KDIGO's individualised A1c goal is < 6.5 % to < 8.0 % (Rec 2.2.1), with CGM time-in-range and GMI as alternatives (PP 2.2.2, 2.1.3) [3]. ADA CGM goals: time in range 70–180 mg/dL > 70 %; time < 70 mg/dL < 4 % (< 1 % if older or high-risk); time < 54 mg/dL < 1 % (Recs 6.3b–c) [9]. **For the log:** record meals, carbohydrate and the CGM pattern around them; the clinic will adjust insulin from time-in-range, not A1c.

## 5. Labels, additives, leaching, eating out, salt substitutes, supplements

### Reading the label

Phosphorus is **not required** on the US Nutrition Facts panel; potassium is. The panel's Daily Values are for healthy adults (sodium 2,300 mg, potassium 4,700 mg, phosphorus 1,250 mg), so % DV for potassium and phosphorus misleads here: read the milligrams [20][38][16].

1. **Ingredient list first, for "PHOS":** phosphoric acid, mono/di/trisodium phosphate, sodium hexametaphosphate, sodium tripolyphosphate, tetrasodium pyrophosphate, sodium acid pyrophosphate, di/tricalcium phosphate, monocalcium phosphate, potassium/dipotassium phosphate, "pyrophosphate", "polyphosphate" [4][20]. Any hit = high phosphorus whatever the number says. Usual carriers: dark colas and pepper sodas, bottled coffee and tea drinks, processed and spreadable cheese, non-dairy creamers [30], instant pudding, cake/pancake/biscuit mixes and baking powder, "enhanced" fresh meat and poultry, deli meats and hot dogs, frozen entrées, pizza, fast food, some "fortified" cereals and plant milks (oat milk especially [28]).
2. **Potassium additives:** potassium chloride (salt substitutes, many "reduced-sodium" soups, broths, sauces and deli meats), potassium lactate, potassium citrate/phosphate/sorbate, acesulfame-K. Additive potassium is ~90 % absorbed, versus 50–60 % from whole plant foods and 70–90 % from animal foods (KDIGO 2024, Figure 33) [2][30].
3. **Milligrams:** potassium ≤ 150 low, 151–250 medium, ≥ 251 high per *your* serving (AKF) [46], NKF's shopping cue ≤ 200 mg [38]; phosphorus ≤ 100 low, ≥ 200 high [46]; sodium **≤ 5 % DV (≤ 115 mg) low, ≥ 20 % DV (≥ 460 mg) high** [34][4]; FDA words: "sodium-free" < 5 mg, "very low" ≤ 35 mg, "low sodium" ≤ 140 mg, "reduced" = 25 % less than the original [34][37]. NKF's rule of thumb: ≤ 240 mg sodium per serving and milligrams of sodium less than calories [38].
4. **Carbohydrate:** Total Carbohydrate × the servings actually eaten (a 20-oz soda is 2.5 servings; cereal bowls are usually 1½). Sugar alcohols are still carbohydrate; the usual teaching is to count about half (erythritol ~0) and watch the CGM [32]. "Diabetic" cookies, bars and shakes often add phosphate or potassium salts [30]. Diet colas still contain phosphoric acid (measured ~27 mg P per 12 oz, 41–68 mg for some brands) [25]; clear diet sodas are phosphate-free [51].
5. **P-to-protein ratio** (if phosphorus is listed): < 10–12 mg per gram of protein is efficient protein (egg white < 2, chicken breast ~7); processed cheese, nuts, beans and colas are not [13][14].

### Potassium leaching (potatoes, sweet potatoes, carrots, beets, winter squash)

NKF method [5]: peel and hold in cold water; slice **⅛ inch** thick; rinse in warm water; soak **at least 2 hours** in warm unsalted water, **10 parts water to 1 part vegetable** (change the water every 4 hours if soaking longer); rinse again; cook in **5 parts fresh unsalted water**; discard the water. What the evidence shows: soaking alone removed essentially no potassium, **boiling 1-cm cubes removed ~50 %**, boiling shredded potato ~75 %, and soaking before boiling added nothing beyond boiling (Bethke & Jansky 2008), so double-boiling small pieces (boil, drain, boil again in fresh water, 20–30 minutes) performs about as well as the long soak [6][32]; KDIGO 2024 likewise notes soaking in previously boiled water "can effectively reduce the potassium by half for some foods" [2]. Baking, roasting, microwaving and frying remove **nothing**. A leached ½ cup of potato is still ~130 mg potassium and still counts.

### Eating out

From NKF's *Dining Out with Confidence* and AKF [48][14]: plan the day around the meal (lighter potassium and sodium at the other meals), look up the menu first, and prefer cook-to-order restaurants. Requests that work: "sauce, dressing or gravy on the side", "no salt, please" on grilled or baked items, "no cheese", "no tomatoes", "green beans or rice instead of the potato", "no MSG, soy or fish sauce". Restaurant protein portions are 4–8 oz, two to three times the 3-oz renal portion: order a half, share, or box half before starting (on dialysis the larger portion may be right). Better: grilled chicken, fish, steak or a plain burger; steamed rice or pasta with oil and garlic or pesto; salad with oil and vinegar; fruit cup, angel food cake, sherbet; water with lemon, non-cola soda, iced tea. Poor: anything cured or smoked, soups, cheese and cream sauces, nachos, potato skins, fries and baked potatoes, beans, tomato sauces, spinach, avocado, melon, oranges, bananas, dried fruit, star fruit, colas, milkshakes, chocolate and nut desserts [48]. Restaurant plates routinely carry 1,000–3,000 mg sodium [35]. **Diabetes:** know the carbohydrate before bolusing (restaurant rice and pasta are often ~90 g), alcohol only with food and with a plan for delayed lows (ADA Recs 5.18–5.19) [8][37], and carry glucose tablets so the rescue is never a cola.

### Salt substitutes

"NoSalt", "Nu-Salt", "Lite Salt", "half salt" and many "low-sodium" soups and broths are **potassium chloride**. NoSalt lists 640 mg potassium per ¼ tsp; Morton Lite Salt 350 mg potassium *plus* 290 mg sodium per ¼ tsp, with a label warning against use on sodium- or potassium-restricted diets without a physician's approval; Nu-Salt 530 mg per labelled serving [35][20]. KCl is ~90 % absorbed, far more than the potassium in fruit [2]. NIDDK, NKF and AKF all say to use herbs, spices, lemon and vinegar instead [4][5]. Garlic *powder*, not garlic *salt*.

### Supplements and over-the-counter products

* **No supplement without the nephrologist's sign-off; renal-specific vitamins only.** NKF lists as especially risky in kidney disease: alfalfa, oral aloe vera, aristolochia, arnica, astragalus, bearberry/uva ursi, cat's claw, chaparral, comfrey, creatine, goldenrod, horsetail, licorice root, nettle, St John's wort, yohimbe; AKF adds barberry, java tea, Oregon grape root, parsley root, pennyroyal and others [41][42]. "Electrolyte", "green" and "superfood" powders, kelp, noni, dandelion and many multivitamins carry hidden potassium, phosphorus or magnesium [41].
* **Sodium phosphate laxatives and enemas (Fleet-type):** FDA warns they can cause acute phosphate nephropathy, dangerous hyperphosphataemia and death, especially with kidney disease, dehydration, diuretics, ACE inhibitors/ARBs or NSAIDs. Avoid in CKD [43].
* **Magnesium laxatives and antacids** (milk of magnesia, magnesium citrate, Maalox/Mylanta-type, Epsom salts) accumulate in CKD and cause life-threatening hypermagnesaemia; **aluminium** antacids and **Alka-Seltzer-type** (high-sodium) products are also unsuitable [44][45]. Constipation itself raises potassium; ask the care team about polyethylene glycol, senna, docusate or lactulose instead [2].

What to notice:

* **It is deliberately light on calories.** KDOQI's 25–35 kcal/kg is ≈ 1,900–2,800 kcal for 75–80 kg [1]; this day is 400–500 kcal under the floor. Close the gap with fat (another tablespoon of olive oil, butter, mayonnaise, a second tablespoon of peanut butter) or more measured starch covered by insulin, **not with more meat**, which would push protein and phosphorus up [3].
* There is ~1,100 mg of sodium headroom and ~900–1,400 mg of potassium headroom for seasoning and a second vegetable, depending on the ceiling in use.
* **Hypo treatments sit on top of this:** two 15-g lows treated with glucose tablets add 0 mg potassium; with apple juice ~250 mg; with orange juice ~500 mg. The app logs them as foods.

## 7. How the app uses this

* **Per-serving warnings** (`medium` / `high`) on every food and log entry: potassium 101–200 mg medium, > 200 mg high (NKF's ≥ 200 mg rule [5]); phosphorus 101–150 mg medium, > 150 mg high, **or any `phosphate_additive` food is high regardless of milligrams** [20]; sodium 141–400 mg medium, > 400 mg high (FDA "low" ≤ 140 mg [34]); carbohydrate 15–30 g medium, > 30 g high, and `high_gi` upgrades to high only from one carb choice (≥ 15 g) upwards (below that the flag is a medium note, so a tablespoon of ketchup or one slice of white bread, the renal swap of section 4, is not red for glycaemic index alone; per-portion glycaemic load, not index, is what matters); protein 15–25 g medium, > 25 g high. The phosphorus line is set at the strict end of the published conventions because every database under-counts additive phosphorus [12][15]. `avoid_ckd` foods (star fruit) always rate high; `hypo_treatment` marks fast carbohydrate that is also low in potassium and **switches the carbohydrate warning off** for that food (glucose tablets rate green; apple juice keeps its moderate-potassium warning so the lowest-potassium rescue can still be chosen). Red = any high warning, yellow = any medium, otherwise green.
* **Daily status** per targeted nutrient: `ok` below 80 % of target (the profile's adjustable `warn_fraction`), `caution` 80–100 %, `over` above 100 %, plus an "if you eat what's planned" projection from planned entries. Hypoglycaemia treatments are logged like any food and **never blocked or warned against**.
* **Suggested targets** (`suggest_targets(weight_kg, ckd_stage, dialysis, diabetes, height_cm)`) come from `docs/research/targets_by_stage.json`, labelled "discuss with your care team". Per kg means per kg of **ideal** body weight, as in section 2 and KDOQI 2020 [1]: with a saved height the app uses the weight at BMI 25 for someone above it and at BMI 18.5 for someone below it (the profile has no sex, so the Hamwi/Devine formulas cannot be used), the actual weight in between, and says so in the first note; without a height it uses the actual weight and says that too. Then: sodium 2,000 mg and calcium 1,000 mg at every stage; potassium review ceilings 4,000 (stages 1–3a) → 3,500 (3b) → 3,000 (4) → 2,500 (5 and hemodialysis), 3,500 on peritoneal dialysis, always with the note *"Only restrict potassium if your blood potassium is high; your care team sets the number"*; phosphorus 1,000 mg (stages 1–4, hemodialysis, peritoneal) and 900 mg at stage 5 pre-dialysis; protein 0.6–0.8 g/kg at stages 3–5 (0.8–1.0 at stages 1–2), 1.0–1.2 on dialysis; 30 kcal/kg with carbohydrate at 45 % of calories split per meal; fluid untracked without dialysis, 1,500 mL on hemodialysis (1,000 mL + an assumed 500 mL of urine) and 2,000 mL on peritoneal dialysis.
* **Periods** follow section 2: potassium, sodium, fluid and carbohydrate are judged **daily**; phosphorus, protein, calories and calcium on the **weekly average** against the previous period; with hemodialysis days set, potassium, sodium and fluid are also totalled **since the last session** against *per-day target × days*, so the long weekend gap is visible [28][53]. Saved meals and "copy a day" let the sample day above serve as a template.
