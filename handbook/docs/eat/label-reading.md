# Reading food labels

## In short

On a US Nutrition Facts label, **phosphorus does not have to be listed; potassium does**. The
label's Daily Values are set for healthy adults (sodium 2,300 mg, potassium 4,700 mg, phosphorus
1,250 mg), so the "% DV" for potassium and phosphorus is misleading for you: read the milligrams
([NKF][DG20]; [NKF label guide][DG38]; [FDA: Daily Values][DG16b]). Always read the **ingredient
list first**.

## The five-step check

- [ ] **1. Ingredient list: look for "PHOS".** Phosphoric acid, mono-, di- or trisodium phosphate,
      sodium hexametaphosphate, sodium tripolyphosphate, tetrasodium pyrophosphate, sodium acid
      pyrophosphate, di- or tricalcium phosphate, monocalcium phosphate, potassium or dipotassium
      phosphate, "pyrophosphate", "polyphosphate" ([NIDDK][DG4]; [NKF][DG20]). Any of these means
      the food is high in phosphorus whatever the number says. See [Phosphate additives](phosphate-additives.md).
- [ ] **2. Ingredient list: look for potassium additives.** Potassium chloride (salt substitutes and
      many "reduced-sodium" soups, broths, sauces and deli meats), potassium lactate, potassium
      citrate, phosphate or sorbate, acesulfame-K. Additive potassium is about 90 % absorbed, versus
      50–60 % from whole plant foods and 70–90 % from animal foods ([KDIGO 2024][K24], Figure 33;
      [Picard 2021][DG30a]; [DaVita][DG30b]).
- [ ] **3. Milligrams per serving you will eat.**
      Potassium: 150 mg or less is low, 151–250 medium, 251 or more high ([AKF][DG46a]); NKF's
      shopping cue is 200 mg or less ([NKF label guide][DG38]).
      Phosphorus (if listed): 100 mg or less is low, 200 or more high ([AKF][DG46b]).
      Sodium: 5 % DV or less (115 mg or less) is low; 20 % DV or more (460 mg or more) is high
      ([FDA][DG34]; [NIDDK][DG4]). FDA words: "sodium-free" under 5 mg, "very low sodium" 35 mg or
      less, "low sodium" 140 mg or less, "reduced sodium" 25 % less than the original
      ([FDA][DG34]; [FDA claim definitions][DG37a]). NKF's rule of thumb: 240 mg or less per
      serving, and fewer milligrams of sodium than calories ([NKF label guide][DG38]).
- [ ] **4. Carbohydrate.** Total Carbohydrate × the servings you actually eat (a 20-oz soda is
      2.5 servings; cereal bowls are usually 1½). Sugar alcohols are still carbohydrate: the usual
      teaching is to count about half (erythritol about none) and watch your CGM ([Medtronic][DG32b]).
      "Diabetic" cookies, bars and shakes often add phosphate or potassium salts ([DaVita][DG30b]).
      Diet colas still contain phosphoric acid (about 27 mg per 12 oz measured, 41–68 mg for some
      brands); clear diet sodas are phosphate-free ([measured beverage phosphorus][DG25b]; [FDC][FDC]).
- [ ] **5. Phosphorus-to-protein ratio** (only if phosphorus is listed): under 10–12 mg per gram of
      protein is an efficient protein (egg white under 2, chicken breast about 7); processed cheese,
      nuts, beans and colas are not ([Noori 2010][DG13a]; [Noori 2010][DG14a]).

## Examples

- **Plant milks:** two unsweetened almond milks can differ a lot. Pick one with no "phosphate" in the
  ingredients and check potassium per cup ([NKF: milk alternatives][DG28b]).
- **"Reduced sodium" broth:** look for potassium chloride in the ingredients before buying.
- **Breakfast cereal:** the label serving may be ¾ cup; if your bowl holds 1½ cups, double every
  number, carbohydrate included.

## Ask your care team

1. Which numbers on the label matter most for me right now: potassium, phosphorus, sodium?
2. Is my favourite plant milk or creamer OK?
3. How should I count sugar alcohols?

## Get help now if…

- Labels do not cause emergencies. For urgent symptoms, see [Get help now](../get-help-now.md).
