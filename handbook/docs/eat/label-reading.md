# Reading food labels

## In short

On a US Nutrition Facts label, **potassium must be listed; phosphorus does not have to be**
([FDA][FDA-label]; [NKF label guide][DG38]). The label's Daily Values are set for healthy adults, so the
"% DV" for potassium and phosphorus is misleading for you: read the milligrams ([NKF][DG20];
[FDA: Daily Values][DG16b]). Always read the **ingredient list first**, because additives are where
the hidden phosphorus and potassium are. Then check the serving size against what you will really eat.

## Your numbers

| Nutrient | Label Daily Value (healthy adults) | What to look for |
|---|---|---|
| Sodium | 2,300 mg | 140 mg or less per serving is "low sodium"; 460 mg (20 % DV) or more is high ([FDA][DG34]) |
| Potassium | 4,700 mg | 150 mg or less is low, 251 mg or more is high, *if* you have a potassium limit ([AKF][DG46a]) |
| Phosphorus | 1,250 mg (often not listed) | any "phos" ingredient means high; 100 mg or less is low, 200 mg or more high ([AKF][DG46b]) |
| Carbohydrate | 275 g | Total Carbohydrate × the servings you eat; this is the number for your insulin plan |

Daily Values from [FDA: Daily Values][DG16b]. **A serving is not a portion:** the serving is the amount
on the label; the portion is what you put on your plate, and the two often differ ([NIDDK][NIDDK-portions]).

## The five-step check

- [ ] **1. Ingredient list: look for "PHOS".** Phosphoric acid, mono-, di- or trisodium phosphate,
      sodium hexametaphosphate, sodium tripolyphosphate, tetrasodium pyrophosphate, sodium acid
      pyrophosphate, di- or tricalcium phosphate, monocalcium phosphate, potassium or dipotassium
      phosphate, "pyrophosphate", "polyphosphate" ([NIDDK][DG4]; [NKF][DG20]). Any of these means the
      food is high in phosphorus whatever the number says. See [Phosphate additives](phosphate-additives.md).
- [ ] **2. Ingredient list: look for potassium additives.** Potassium chloride (salt substitutes and many
      "reduced-sodium" soups, broths, sauces and deli meats), potassium lactate, potassium citrate,
      phosphate or sorbate, acesulfame-K. Additive potassium is about 90 % absorbed, versus 50–60 % from
      whole plant foods and 70–90 % from animal foods ([KDIGO 2024][K24], Figure 33; [Picard 2021][DG30a];
      [DaVita][DG30b]). On fresh meat and poultry, also look for "enhanced" or "contains up to X % of a
      solution": in one study 8 of 25 enhanced products did not list their additives
      ([Sherman and Mehta 2009][SHERMAN09]).
- [ ] **3. Milligrams per serving you will eat.**
      Potassium: 150 mg or less is low, 151–250 medium, 251 or more high ([AKF][DG46a]); NKF's shopping
      cue is 200 mg or less ([NKF label guide][DG38]).
      Phosphorus (if listed): 100 mg or less is low, 200 or more high ([AKF][DG46b]).
      Sodium: 5 % DV or less (115 mg or less) is low; 20 % DV or more (460 mg or more) is high
      ([FDA][DG34]; [NIDDK][DG4]). FDA words: "sodium-free" under 5 mg, "very low sodium" 35 mg or
      less, "low sodium" 140 mg or less, "reduced sodium" 25 % less than the original
      ([FDA][DG34]; [FDA claim definitions][DG37a]). NKF's rule of thumb: 240 mg or less per serving,
      and fewer milligrams of sodium than calories ([NKF label guide][DG38]).
- [ ] **4. Carbohydrate.** Total Carbohydrate × the servings you actually eat. Check the serving
      first: a package of one to two servings, such as a 20-oz soda or a 15-oz can of soup, is now
      labelled as **one** serving, so its numbers already cover the whole bottle or can; bigger packages
      may show a second column for the whole package ([FDA][FDA-label]). A bowl of cereal is often 1½–2
      label servings. Sugar alcohols are still carbohydrate: one common method is to subtract half of
      them from Total Carbohydrate ([Medtronic][DG32b]); ask your diabetes team which method to use and
      watch your CGM.
      "Diabetic" cookies, bars and shakes often add phosphate or potassium salts ([DaVita][DG30b]).
      Diet colas still contain phosphoric acid (about 27 mg per 12 oz measured, 41–68 mg for some
      brands); clear diet sodas are phosphate-free ([measured beverage phosphorus][DG25b]; [FDC][FDC]).
- [ ] **5. Phosphorus-to-protein ratio** (only if phosphorus is listed): under 10–12 mg per gram of
      protein is an efficient protein (egg white under 2, chicken breast about 7); processed cheese,
      nuts, beans and colas are not ([Noori 2010][DG13a]; [Noori 2010][DG14a]).

## Worked example: a can of chicken noodle soup

These are typical USDA values for canned chicken noodle soup, prepared, per 1-cup serving
([FDC][FDC]). A can of condensed soup is labelled as more than one serving, so a full bowl may well
be 2 cups. (A ready-to-serve can of up to about 2 servings is labelled as one serving, so read the
serving line first: [FDA][FDA-label].)

| Label line | Per serving (1 cup) | If you eat 2 cups | What it tells you |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sodium | 831 mg (36 % DV) | about 1,660 mg | high: 20 % DV or more. Two cups are most of a 2,000 mg day |
| Potassium | 60 mg (1 % DV) | about 120 mg | low, unless the ingredients list potassium chloride |
| Total Carbohydrate | 7 g | about 15 g | about 1 carbohydrate choice for 2 cups |
| Protein | 3 g | about 6 g | not much protein for a meal |
| Ingredients | | | check for "sodium phosphate" in the chicken and "potassium chloride" in "reduced-sodium" versions |

The verdict: a cup now and then fits; a 2-cup bowl uses most of the day's sodium. The [home-made chicken noodle
soup](recipes/chicken-noodle-soup.md) has about 130 mg sodium in a 1½-cup bowl. Soup also counts as
fluid ([Fluid](fluid.md)).

## More examples

- **Plant milks:** two unsweetened almond milks can differ a lot. Pick one with no "phosphate" in the
  ingredients and check potassium per cup ([NKF: milk alternatives][DG28b]).
- **"Reduced sodium" broth:** look for potassium chloride in the ingredients before buying.
- **Breakfast cereal:** the label serving may be ¾ cup; if your bowl holds 1½ cups, double every number,
  carbohydrate included.

## Labels outside the US

UK and EU labels list **salt**, not sodium, usually per 100 g and per portion. Salt = sodium × 2.5, so
divide the salt figure by 2.5 to get sodium: 1 g of salt is 400 mg of sodium
([EU regulation 1169/2011][EU1169]). Potassium and phosphorus are usually not listed, so the ingredient
list (steps 1 and 2) matters even more. Additives may appear as E-numbers: phosphates are in the E338–E343
and E450–E452 range, and potassium chloride is E508 ([design note 03][NOTE03]).

!!! tip "In the app"
    If barcode scanning is turned on, a scan shows the label's numbers and flags phosphate or potassium
    additives it finds in the ingredient list. A missing flag does not prove a food is additive-free, so
    read the list yourself ([design note 03][NOTE03]). You can also enter a label's numbers yourself as a
    custom food ([Logging food](../app/logging.md); [Barcodes and label photos](../app/barcode-and-photo.md)).

## Ask your care team

1. Which numbers on the label matter most for me right now: potassium, phosphorus, sodium?
2. Is my favourite plant milk or creamer OK?
3. How should I count sugar alcohols with my CGM?
4. Can a dietitian go through the labels of the foods I buy most?

## Get help now if…

Labels do not cause emergencies. If you feel a low coming on while shopping or reading labels, treat it
first ([Treating a low](../t1d/treating-a-low.md)). For urgent symptoms, see [Get help now](../get-help-now.md).
