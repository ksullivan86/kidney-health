# Sodium

## In short

Sodium holds water in the body. When the kidneys cannot get rid of it, the extra water raises blood
pressure, causes swelling, increases protein in the urine and, on dialysis, makes you thirsty enough to
break a fluid limit ([KDOQI 2020][Q20]; [KDIGO 2024][K24]). Aim for **under 2,000 mg a day** at every
stage. Most sodium comes from packaged and restaurant food, not the salt shaker. **Salt substitutes
contain potassium chloride and are not safe without your team's OK.**

## Your numbers

--8<-- "includes/starting-points.md"

=== "US units"

    | | Amount |
    |---|---|
    | Daily target | under 2,000 mg sodium |
    | 1 teaspoon of table salt | about 2,300 mg sodium |
    | Average US intake | about 3,400 mg a day |
    | "Low sodium" on a label | 140 mg or less per serving |
    | A restaurant meal | often 1,000–3,000 mg |

=== "International"

    | | Amount |
    |---|---|
    | Daily target | under 2,000 mg sodium = under 90 mmol = under 5 g of salt |
    | Salt on a UK or EU label | salt (g) = sodium (g) × 2.5, so 1 g of salt has 400 mg sodium |
    | 1 teaspoon of table salt | about 6 g of salt |

Sources: KDIGO 2024 recommendation 3.3.2.1 and KDIGO 2022 ([KDIGO 2024][K24]; [KDIGO 2022][K22]); salt
formula from [EU regulation 1169/2011][EU1169]; teaspoon from [FDC][FDC]; US intake, label word and
restaurant range from [FDA][DG34], [FDA claim definitions][DG37a] and [Harvard Health][DG35a]. Some
guidelines say under 2,300 mg (KDOQI 2020 statement 6.5.1; NIDDK; NKF on hemodialysis) ([KDOQI 2020][Q20];
[NIDDK][DG4]; [NKF][DG24a]). The app uses 2,000 mg.

**Why it works.** In trials lasting up to 36 weeks, eating less sodium lowered blood pressure and
albumin in the urine in people with CKD ([KDIGO 2024][K24]). On hemodialysis, salt is the main driver of
thirst, so it decides how much fluid you gain between sessions ([DaVita][DG23]; [Fluid](fluid.md)).

**Where it comes from.** More than 70 % of the sodium people eat comes from packaged and prepared food
([FDA][DG34]). The top sources in the US include deli-meat sandwiches, pizza, burritos and tacos, soups,
savory snacks, poultry, pasta dishes, burgers and egg dishes ([FDA][DG34]). Your taste for salt fades
over time as you eat less, "so eventually, you may not even miss it" ([FDA][DG34]).

## Reading the label

| On the label | Means |
|---|---|
| 5 % DV or less (115 mg or less) | low |
| 20 % DV or more (460 mg or more) | high |
| "Sodium-free" | under 5 mg per serving |
| "Very low sodium" | 35 mg or less |
| "Low sodium" | 140 mg or less |
| "Reduced sodium" | 25 % less than the original, which can still be a lot |

([FDA][DG34]; [FDA claim definitions][DG37a]; [NIDDK][DG4].) NKF's rule of thumb: 240 mg or less per
serving, and fewer milligrams of sodium than calories ([NKF label guide][DG38]). The label's Daily Value
for sodium is 2,300 mg, more than your target. Outside the US, labels list **salt**: divide by 2.5 to get
sodium ([EU regulation 1169/2011][EU1169]).

## Salt substitutes

"NoSalt", "Nu-Salt", "Lite Salt", "half salt" and many "low-sodium" soups and broths are made with
**potassium chloride**. NoSalt lists 640 mg potassium per ¼ teaspoon, and its label tells people with
diabetes or kidney disease to ask a doctor first; Nu-Salt lists 530 mg per labeled serving
([NoSalt and Nu-Salt labels][SALTSUB-LABELS]). Morton Lite Salt is half salt: 350 mg potassium *plus*
290 mg sodium per ¼ teaspoon, with a label warning against use on sodium- or potassium-restricted diets
without a doctor's approval ([Morton Lite Salt label][DG35b]). NKF lists salt substitutes and lite
salt among high-potassium foods ([NKF][DG5]). Potassium from potassium chloride is about 90 % absorbed, far more than the potassium in
fruit ([KDIGO 2024][K24]). Kidney groups have opposed allowing potassium-chloride salt substitutes in
standard foods for this reason ([AAKP letter][DG33]). NIDDK suggests spices, herbs and sodium-free
seasonings instead ([NIDDK][DG4]); lemon and vinegar work too. Use garlic *powder*, not garlic *salt*.

## What to do

- [ ] Cook at home more often, from fresh food.
- [ ] Take the salt shaker off the table; season with herbs, spices, pepper, lemon and vinegar.
- [ ] Use a salt-free blend **only** if its label does not list potassium chloride.
- [ ] Check the label: 140 mg or less per serving is "low sodium"; 460 mg or more is high.
- [ ] Rinse canned vegetables, beans and tuna, and choose "no salt added" versions ([FDA][DG34]).
- [ ] Cook rice, pasta and vegetables without salt.
- [ ] Go easy on sauces: soy sauce, ketchup, barbecue sauce, gravy and bouillon cubes.
- [ ] When eating out, ask for "no salt, please" and sauces on the side ([Eating out](eating-out.md)).

## Ask your care team

1. Is my target under 2,000 mg or under 2,300 mg?
2. Is my blood pressure where you want it? Should I check it at home?
3. Is any salt substitute safe for me? (Usually the answer is no.)
4. On dialysis: how much is my thirst affecting my fluid gains?

## Get help now if…

!!! danger "Call 911 (or your local emergency number)"
    You are suddenly very short of breath, cannot lie flat, have a frothy cough or chest pain. These can
    be signs of fluid overload.

!!! warning "Call your care team today"
    New swelling of your legs or face, much less urine, or weight gain above your team's alarm number.
    Full list: [Get help now](../get-help-now.md).
