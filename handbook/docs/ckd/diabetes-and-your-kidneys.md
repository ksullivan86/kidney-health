# Type 1 diabetes and your kidneys

--8<-- "includes/no-dosing.md"

## In short

Over many years, high glucose damages the tiny blood vessels that filter your blood, and **albumin
starts to leak into the urine**. That leak is the earliest sign, long before your eGFR falls or you
feel anything ([NIDDK][NIDDK-DKD]; [ADA 2026 §11][A26-11]). In type 1 diabetes, kidney disease usually
appears after about 10 years, most often 5 to 15 years after diagnosis ([ADA 2026 §11][A26-11]).
About 1 in 3 adults with diabetes has kidney disease, but most never reach kidney failure
([NIDDK][NIDDK-DKD]). The things that slow it are well proven: glucose in range, lower blood pressure,
an ACE inhibitor or ARB when albumin is present, not smoking and staying active. Since September 2026,
**finerenone** is also FDA-approved for kidney disease with type 1 diabetes ([Kerendia label][KER]).

## Medicines that protect the kidneys

**ACE inhibitors and ARBs** lower the pressure inside the kidney filters and cut the albumin leak.
KDIGO recommends one for people with diabetes and a UACR of 30 mg/g (3 mg/mmol) or more
([KDIGO 2024][K24], recommendation 3.6.3; [KDIGO 2022][K22], recommendation 1.2.1). What to expect:

- Blood pressure, creatinine and potassium are checked **2–4 weeks** after starting or after a dose
  change ([KDIGO 2024][K24], practice point 3.6.2).
- A **small early drop in eGFR is expected** and is not a reason to stop. KDIGO says to keep going
  unless creatinine rises by more than 30 % within 4 weeks (practice point 3.6.4).
- A higher potassium can often be managed without stopping the medicine (practice point 3.6.3), and
  the medicine is usually continued even when eGFR falls below 30 (practice point 3.6.7).
- If you could become pregnant, ask about pregnancy safety and birth control **before** you start.
  KDIGO asks clinicians to check every medicine for this ([KDIGO 2024][K24], practice point 4.1.4;
  [Sex, fertility and pregnancy](../living/sex-fertility-pregnancy.md)).

**Finerenone (Kerendia)** was approved by the FDA in September 2026 "to reduce urinary
albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR)" in adults with CKD and type 1 diabetes ([Kerendia label][KER]).

- In the FINE-ONE trial, 242 adults who were already taking an ACE inhibitor or ARB took finerenone
  or a dummy pill for 6 months. UACR fell 34 % with finerenone and 12 % with the dummy pill, a 25 %
  greater fall ([FINE-ONE][F1]).
- **High potassium** was the most common side effect: 10.1 % of people on finerenone and 3.3 % on the
  dummy pill. eGFR dipped a little more with finerenone and came back toward its starting level after
  the medicine was stopped ([FINE-ONE][F1]).
- Potassium and eGFR are checked before starting, and it is **not started if potassium is above
  5.0 mmol/L**. **Avoid grapefruit and grapefruit juice.** Some other medicines, such as certain
  antifungal pills, must not be taken with it, so tell every prescriber and pharmacist
  ([Kerendia label][KER]).

**SGLT2 inhibitors** (for example empagliflozin or dapagliflozin) protect the kidneys in type 2
diabetes, but their safety and benefit **have not been established in type 1 diabetes**, and the FDA
has not approved them for it ([KDIGO 2022][K22], chapter 1). In Europe, dapagliflozin was approved
for some adults with type 1 diabetes in 2019, and its maker withdrew that use in 2021. The UK
regulator said this was not because of a new safety problem, but diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) was
common in the type 1 diabetes trials, affecting up to 1 in 10 people ([MHRA][MHRA-DAPA]).
KDIGO still lists people with type 1 diabetes as not well studied ([KDIGO 2024][K24], research
recommendations). In trials in type 1 diabetes, a fasting ketone level of 0.8 mmol/L or more meant a
3.2 times higher chance of DKA in the next month ([ADA 2026 §6][A26-6]). DKA can happen even when
glucose is not very high ([2024 consensus][HC24]). If a specialist prescribes one off-label,
you need a blood ketone meter, a written sick-day plan, and ketone checks whenever you feel unwell,
whatever your glucose. See [Kidney-protecting medicines](../t1d/kidney-protecting-medicines.md) and
[Sick days](../t1d/sick-days.md).

**Statins** (cholesterol medicines) protect the heart, the biggest risk in CKD. KDIGO recommends one
for adults aged 50 or over with CKD who are not on dialysis, whatever their eGFR, and suggests one
for adults aged 18–49 with diabetes and CKD who are not on dialysis
([KDIGO 2024][K24], recommendations 3.15.1.1–3.15.1.3).

## When to suspect another cause

Not every kidney problem in someone with diabetes comes from diabetes. ADA lists these signs that
something else may be going on ([ADA 2026 §11][A26-11]):

- blood cells or "casts" in the urine (an active urine sediment);
- albumin or protein in the urine that rises fast, or very heavy protein loss (nephrotic syndrome);
- an eGFR that falls fast;
- **no diabetic eye disease (retinopathy)**, which points away from diabetes, especially in type 1.

Kidney disease that shows up in the first few years after diagnosis is also unusual, because it
typically takes about 10 years ([ADA 2026 §11][A26-11]). If any of these apply, ask whether you need a
nephrologist to look for another cause. ADA also advises a nephrology referral when eGFR is under 30,
when the cause is unclear, or when albumin rises or eGFR falls quickly (recommendations
11.12a–11.12b).

## What to do

- [ ] Get your **UACR and eGFR** at least once a year ([ADA 2026 §11][A26-11], recommendation 11.1a).
- [ ] Know your **blood pressure goal**, and check at home if your team asks you to.
- [ ] Ask whether you should be on an **ACE inhibitor or ARB**, and whether **finerenone** fits you.
- [ ] Keep your **yearly eye exam**: the result helps your team judge whether diabetes is the cause.
- [ ] Use a **CGM** if you can, and share the report at visits ([CGM metrics](../labs/cgm-metrics.md)).
- [ ] Stop smoking; ask for help to quit ([KDIGO 2022][K22], recommendation 1.5.1).
- [ ] Aim for 150 minutes of activity a week ([Exercise](../living/exercise.md)).
- [ ] Keep sodium under 2,000 mg a day ([Sodium](../eat/sodium.md)) and protein near your goal
      ([Protein](../eat/protein.md)).
- [ ] Avoid ibuprofen, naproxen and other NSAIDs unless your team says otherwise
      ([Medicines to avoid](../medicines/avoid.md)).
- [ ] Have a written **sick-day plan** ([Sick days](../t1d/sick-days.md)).

## Examples

- **Sam, 34, type 1 diabetes for 18 years.** UACR 85 mg/g (10 mg/mmol) on two tests, eGFR 78, mild
  retinopathy. He has CKD G2 A2 from diabetes. His team starts an ARB, sets a blood pressure goal and
  rechecks potassium and creatinine in 3 weeks. Six months later his UACR is 40 mg/g.
- **Rosa, 29, type 1 diabetes for 4 years.** UACR 900 mg/g, blood in the urine and no retinopathy.
  The short time since diagnosis, the blood and the clear eyes all point away from diabetes, so she is
  referred to a nephrologist to look for another cause.
- **Dev, 52, type 1 diabetes, CKD G3b A3, on an ACE inhibitor.** His potassium is 4.6 mmol/L. His nephrologist adds
  finerenone, tells him to stop drinking grapefruit juice, and checks potassium again after starting.

## Ask your care team

1. Is my kidney disease from diabetes, or could there be another cause?
2. What blood pressure goal is right for me, and should I check it at home?
3. Should I be on an ACE inhibitor or ARB? If I am, is it at the dose that protects my kidneys?
4. Is finerenone right for me? How often would you check my potassium?
5. What A1c or time-in-range goal is safe for me at this stage?
6. Should I take a statin?

## Get help now if…

- You have a low you cannot treat yourself, ketones of 1.6 mmol/L or more, or a lab potassium of
  6.0 mmol/L or more: see [Get help now](../get-help-now.md).
- On finerenone or an ACE inhibitor or ARB: new weakness, a slow or irregular pulse or fainting can
  mean high potassium. Call 911.
