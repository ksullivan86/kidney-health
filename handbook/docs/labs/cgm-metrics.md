# CGM metrics

## In short

A continuous glucose monitor (CGM) shows how much of the day you spend in range, low and high. With
advanced kidney disease, when A1c becomes unreliable, these numbers are the main way your team judges
your glucose ([KDIGO 2022][K22]; [A1c](a1c.md)). **Look at the lows first**: kidney disease makes lows
more likely and harder to feel ([JBDS–Renal Association][JBDS]). On dialysis, CGM makers say their
sensors have not been tested in people on dialysis, so check any low or surprising reading with a
fingerstick meter.

## Your numbers

=== "US units"

    | Metric | Goal for most adults | Older or higher-risk adults |
    |---|---|---|
    | Time in range, 70–180 mg/dL | more than 70 % (16 h 48 min a day) | more than 50 % (12 h) |
    | Time below 70 mg/dL | less than 4 % (about 1 h) | less than 1 % (about 15 min) |
    | Time below 54 mg/dL | less than 1 % (about 15 min) | |
    | Time above 180 mg/dL | less than 25 % (6 h) | |
    | Time above 250 mg/dL | less than 5 % (1 h 12 min) | less than 10 % (2 h 24 min) |
    | Glucose variability (CV) | 36 % or less | |

=== "International"

    | Metric | Goal for most adults | Older or higher-risk adults |
    |---|---|---|
    | Time in range, 3.9–10.0 mmol/L | more than 70 % (16 h 48 min a day) | more than 50 % (12 h) |
    | Time below 3.9 mmol/L | less than 4 % (about 1 h) | less than 1 % (about 15 min) |
    | Time below 3.0 mmol/L | less than 1 % (about 15 min) | |
    | Time above 10.0 mmol/L | less than 25 % (6 h) | |
    | Time above 13.9 mmol/L | less than 5 % (1 h 12 min) | less than 10 % (2 h 24 min) |
    | Glucose variability (CV) | 36 % or less | |

Use 14 days of data with the sensor working at least 70 % of the time ([time-in-range consensus][TIR19];
[ADA 2026 §6][A26-6], recommendations 6.3b–6.3c). Each 1 % of the day is about 15 minutes. **Every 5 %
more time in range brings real health benefits**, so small steps count ([time-in-range consensus][TIR19]).
As a guide, 70 % in range goes with an A1c of about 7 %, and 50 % with about 8 %. The "older or
higher-risk" column often applies with advanced kidney disease; ask your team which column is yours.

## Reading your AGP report, step by step

Most CGM apps can make an **AGP** (ambulatory glucose profile) report: a one-page, 14-day summary
([time-in-range consensus][TIR19]).

1. **Check the data.** Does it cover about 14 days, with the sensor active 70 % of the time or more?
   If not, the numbers may mislead.
2. **Read the time-in-ranges bar** from the bottom up: very low (below 54 mg/dL, 3.0 mmol/L), low
   (54–69 mg/dL), in range, high (181–250 mg/dL) and very high (above 250 mg/dL, 13.9 mmol/L).
   Compare each with the goals above. **Lows come first**: fix them before chasing highs.
3. **Read the statistics:** average glucose, GMI (an A1c-like number worked out from your CGM) and CV
   (how much your glucose swings; 36 % or less is the goal).
4. **Read the AGP curve.** The middle line is your typical (median) glucose at each time of day; the
   shaded bands show how widely it varies. A band that dips below the low line at the same time most
   days is a pattern worth fixing.
5. **Look at the daily profiles** at the bottom. Compare different kinds of days: dialysis days with
   non-dialysis days, work days with weekends, exercise days with rest days.
6. **Write down 1–3 questions** for your visit, for example "Why am I low most afternoons after
   dialysis?"

## Dialysis days on the report

- **Hemodialysis.** Glucose can swing during a session and most often drops in the last hour. The
  lowest readings usually come **after** dialysis, and lows in the hours after a session are common and
  often not felt ([JBDS–Renal Association][JBDS]). On the daily profiles this shows as a dip during or
  after your session times. UK guidance says glucose should be checked before and after dialysis for
  people on insulin ([JBDS–Renal Association][JBDS]).
- **Peritoneal dialysis.** Glucose in the dialysis fluid is absorbed (often 400 calories a day or
  more) and can raise your glucose ([DaVita][DG25a]). If you
  use **icodextrin (Extraneal)**, some fingerstick meters read falsely **high**, even up to two weeks
  after stopping; use a glucose-specific meter, and ask whether your CGM is compatible
  ([MHRA][MHRA]; [Extraneal label][EXTRANEAL]).
- **Insulin on dialysis days is your team's job.** Show them the pattern; never change doses on your
  own ([Insulin and dialysis](../t1d/insulin-and-dialysis.md)).

## CGM accuracy on dialysis

- **What the makers say.** Dexcom (G7) and Abbott (FreeStyle Libre 3 Plus and 2 Plus) state that their
  systems have not been evaluated in people on dialysis, and that readings may be inaccurate
  ([Dexcom G7 user guide][DEXCOM-G7]; [Abbott safety information][LIBRE-ISI]). Many people on dialysis
  still use a CGM with their team's agreement; ask yours.
- **The time lag is longer.** CGM measures glucose in the fluid under the skin, which trails blood
  glucose. That lag is longer in kidney failure, so a CGM can be late to show a low
  ([JBDS–Renal Association][JBDS]). UK guidance suggests starting a new sensor on a non-dialysis day.
- **Use a meter when in doubt.** Both makers say to use a blood glucose meter when the sensor reading
  does not match how you feel ([Dexcom G7 user guide][DEXCOM-G7]; [Abbott safety information][LIBRE-ISI]).
  ADA says CGM accuracy should be confirmed with a meter when readings and the clinical picture do not
  match ([ADA 2026 §7][A26-7]).
- **Things that can make sensors read falsely high:** hydroxyurea (Dexcom, Medtronic Guardian 4),
  acetaminophen (paracetamol: at any amount for some sensors, above a set amount for others), and
  high-dose vitamin C (FreeStyle Libre) ([ADA 2026 §7][A26-7]). Check your own sensor's list and tell your team what you take.

## What to do

- [ ] Bring a 14-day AGP report to every diabetes and kidney visit.
- [ ] Turn on low alerts, especially at night; most CGMs can alarm for lows and fast falls
      ([KDIGO 2022][K22]).
- [ ] **Treat a low straight away**, whatever the CGM says about the trend
      ([Treating a low](../t1d/treating-a-low.md)).
- [ ] On dialysis, check a low or any surprising reading with a fingerstick meter, and check before and
      after each session if your team asks.
- [ ] On peritoneal dialysis with icodextrin, use only a glucose-specific meter.
- [ ] Mark your dialysis times so you and your team can compare dialysis and non-dialysis days
      ([Dialysis days](../eat/dialysis-days.md)).

## Examples

- **Reading the bar.** Sam's report: 62 % in range, 6 % below 70 mg/dL and 2 % below 54. The lows are
  over goal (4 % and 1 %), so the first job is to reduce them, even though time in range is also
  below 70 %.
- **The post-dialysis dip.** Ella's daily profiles show a drop to the low 60s mg/dL between 3 and 5 pm
  on Monday, Wednesday and Friday, after her morning sessions. She brings this to her diabetes team
  rather than changing anything herself.
- **The mismatch.** On hemodialysis, Raj's CGM reads 95 mg/dL but he feels shaky and sweaty. His meter
  reads 58 mg/dL. He treats the low first, then reports the mismatch.

## Ask your care team

1. Which goals are right for me: the standard or the higher-risk column?
2. Is my CGM accurate enough on dialysis, and when should I double-check with a meter?
3. Does anything I take interfere with my CGM or meter?
4. What pattern do you see on my dialysis days?
5. Can my CGM data be shared with the clinic before visits?

## Get help now if…

!!! danger "Call 911 (or your local emergency number)"
    A low the person cannot treat themselves: give glucagon if you have it, turn them on their side,
    then call. See [Treating a low](../t1d/treating-a-low.md) and [Get help now](../get-help-now.md).

!!! warning "Call your care team today"
    Lows that keep coming back, lows at night, or lows you no longer feel coming
    ([ADA 2026 §6][A26-6]); time below 54 mg/dL (3.0 mmol/L) above 1 % on your report.
