# Reading your lab results

## In short

Blood and urine tests show how well your kidneys filter and whether problems that kidney disease
causes, such as high potassium or anaemia, are starting. Each result has a number, a unit and the
lab's normal range. One result is a snapshot. The **trend** over several tests tells you much more:
KDIGO says an eGFR change of more than 20 % or a doubling of urine albumin is bigger than normal
day-to-day noise and should be looked at ([KDIGO 2024][K24], practice points 2.1.3 and 2.1.5). Keep
your results in one place and bring them to every visit.

## How to read a lab report

| Part of the report | What it means | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Test name | Often shortened: K for potassium, Cr for creatinine, Hgb for haemoglobin, CO2 for bicarbonate | "Potassium, serum" |
| Result | Your number | 5.3 |
| Unit | What the number is counted in. The same test can use different units in different countries | mmol/L (the same number as mEq/L for potassium) |
| Reference range | The range found in healthy people **at that lab**. Labs use different machines, so ranges differ a little | 3.5–5.1 |
| Flag | H = high, L = low. Some labs mark a "critical" result and phone your doctor | H |
| Date and time | When the blood was taken. Compare results taken the same way, for example before dialysis | 2026-09-14, 08:10 |

1. Check the **date**, so you know you are looking at the newest result.
2. Check the **unit** before you compare with a target. To switch units, see
   [Units and conversions](../reference/units.md).
3. Compare with **the lab's own range** printed next to it. Your lab's range wins over any range in
   this handbook.
4. Compare with **your last result**. Is it going up, down or staying the same?
5. Note anything that might have changed the result: an illness, a missed insulin dose, a big meat
   meal, hard exercise, your period, a new medicine. Each lab page lists what can fool that test.

## Trends beat single values

Results bounce around a little even when nothing has changed. These are the changes guidelines treat
as real:

| Test | A change worth asking about | Source |
|---|---|---|
| eGFR | a fall of more than 20 % from one test to the next | [KDIGO 2024][K24], practice point 2.1.3 |
| eGFR after starting an ACE inhibitor, ARB, finerenone or an SGLT2 inhibitor | a fall of more than 30 %; a smaller dip is expected | [KDIGO 2024][K24], practice points 2.1.4 and 3.6.4 |
| UACR (urine albumin) | doubling | [KDIGO 2024][K24], practice point 2.1.5 |
| Calcium, phosphate and PTH | decisions are based on trends, not one value | [KDIGO 2017][K17], recommendation 3.1.4 |
| Potassium | it can swing 0.24–0.73 mmol/L within one day, so a surprise result is often repeated | [KDIGO 2024][K24], Table 25 |

**Worked example.** Your eGFR was 48. A drop to 44 is 8 % lower: within normal noise. A drop to 37
is 23 % lower: more than 20 %, so ask your team what changed. (Work it out: 48 − 37 = 11, and
11 ÷ 48 = 0.23, or 23 %.)

## Which tests, and how often

These are minimum schedules from guidelines. Your team may test more often, especially after a
medicine change or when a result is off.

| Test | Stage 3 (G3a–G3b) | Stage 4 (G4) | Stage 5 and dialysis | Page |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| eGFR and UACR | 1–4 times a year, depending on stage | 1–4 times a year | as your team decides; monthly blood tests on dialysis | [eGFR](egfr-and-creatinine.md), [UACR](uacr.md) |
| Potassium | with each kidney check, and 2–4 weeks after a new or bigger dose of an ACE inhibitor or ARB | same | monthly on dialysis | [Blood potassium](blood-potassium.md) |
| Calcium and phosphate | every 6–12 months | every 3–6 months | every 1–3 months | [Phosphate, calcium and PTH](phosphate-calcium-pth.md) |
| PTH | depends on your first result | every 6–12 months | every 3–6 months | same |
| Haemoglobin | at least once a year | at least twice a year | at least every 3 months | [Haemoglobin and iron](haemoglobin-and-iron.md) |
| A1c | twice a year; up to 4 times if not at goal or after a change | same, but less reliable | same, but unreliable | [A1c](a1c.md), [CGM metrics](cgm-metrics.md) |

Sources: eGFR and UACR ([ADA 2026 §11][A26-11], recommendation 11.1b; [KDIGO 2024][K24], practice
points 2.1.1–2.1.2); potassium after ACE inhibitors and ARBs ([KDIGO 2024][K24], practice point 3.6.2)
and 4 weeks after starting finerenone ([Kerendia label][KER]); calcium, phosphate and PTH
([KDIGO 2017][K17], recommendation 3.1.2); haemoglobin ([KDIGO 2026 anemia][K26A], Figure 5);
A1c ([KDIGO 2022][K22], practice point 2.1.1); monthly tests on dialysis ([NIDDK][NIDDK-HD]).

## Does age or sex change my numbers?

- **eGFR: yes.** The equation uses your creatinine, age and sex, because creatinine comes from muscle
  and muscle mass differs by age and sex ([NKF][NKF-CKDEPI]). The same creatinine of 1.4 mg/dL at
  age 60 gives an eGFR of about 43 for a woman and 57 for a man. Normal creatinine is also lower in
  women ([MedlinePlus][MEDLINE-CREAT]).
- **Anaemia: yes.** It starts below 13 g/dL in men and below 12 g/dL in women
  ([KDIGO 2026 anemia][K26A]).
- **Urine albumin: a little.** Women pass less creatinine, so their UACR reads a bit higher for the
  same albumin ([KDIGO 2024][K24], Table 16).
- **Potassium and bicarbonate: hardly.** Average potassium differs by 0.2 mmol/L or less by age and
  sex ([KDIGO 2024][K24], Table 24).
- **Food limits: no.** Potassium, phosphorus and sodium limits do not change with age or sex. Age, sex,
  height and activity change your calorie needs, and being 65 or older can raise protein
  ([design note 05][NOTE05]). See [Targets and warnings](../app/targets-and-warnings.md).
- **Pregnancy and children: different rules.** The eGFR equations were not built for pregnancy, and
  children use other equations ([KDIGO 2024][K24], practice point 1.2.4.3). Ask your specialist team;
  see [Sex, fertility and pregnancy](../living/sex-fertility-pregnancy.md).

## What to do

- [ ] Ask for a copy of every result, or sign up for your clinic's patient portal.
- [ ] Write each result on the tracker below or add it in the app, with the date.
- [ ] Before a blood test, ask whether to fast. If creatinine is being checked, skip a big meat or fish
      meal for about 12 hours before if you can ([KDIGO 2024][K24], practice point 1.2.2.4).
- [ ] At the blood draw, keep your hand relaxed: pumping your fist can make potassium read falsely high
      ([KDIGO 2024][K24], Table 25).
- [ ] Bring the tracker to every visit and ask "What changed, and why?"
- [ ] Track the trend, not one number. Your team decides treatment from several results.

!!! tip "In the app"
    Add results under **Labs**: potassium, phosphate, albumin, bicarbonate, UACR, creatinine,
    cystatin C, eGFR and A1c. The app converts units, keeps a history, shows a red alert for a
    potassium of 6.0 mmol/L or more, and may suggest that you review your food targets. It never
    changes your stage or your targets by itself ([design note 05][NOTE05]). See
    [Targets and warnings](../app/targets-and-warnings.md) and
    [Reports for your care team](../app/reports-for-your-team.md).

## Ask your care team

1. Which of my results are outside my lab's range, and do any need action?
2. How has my eGFR changed over the last year, as a percentage?
3. Which tests should I expect at my stage, and how often?
4. Should I fast or avoid anything before my next blood test?
5. Can I get my results through a patient portal?

## Get help now if…

!!! danger "Call 911 (or your local emergency number)"
    A lab or your team tells you your **potassium is 6.5 mmol/L or more**, or you have chest pain,
    severe breathlessness, fainting or a very slow or irregular pulse ([KDIGO 2024][K24], Table 28).
    Full list: [Get help now](../get-help-now.md).

!!! warning "Call your care team today"
    A potassium of **6.0–6.4 mmol/L**: KDIGO says get checked in hospital if you feel unwell,
    otherwise repeat the test within 24 hours ([KDIGO 2024][K24], Table 28). Also call if the lab or
    your team phones you about a result and you are not sure what to do.
