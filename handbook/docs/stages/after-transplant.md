# After a kidney transplant

## In short

A working transplant means no dialysis, and more choice about what you eat than on dialysis
([NIDDK][NIDDK-Tx]). The price is **anti-rejection medicines every day, on time, for as long as the
kidney works** ([NKF][NKF-Tx]). Not taking them as prescribed is linked to a high risk of rejection
([KDIGO 2009 transplant][K09TX], chapter 11). These
medicines also raise the risk of infection and skin cancer, can raise blood glucose and potassium, and
interact with grapefruit and some herbal products ([Prograf label][PROGRAF]). With type 1 diabetes,
expect your insulin needs to change; after a successful pancreas-kidney transplant, glucose may return
to normal ([ADA 2026 §9][A26-9]).

### Blood tests for the new kidney

KDIGO recommends checking creatinine at least ([KDIGO 2009 transplant][K09TX], recommendation 8.3):

| Time after transplant | Creatinine check |
|---|---|
| First week, or until you go home | daily |
| Weeks 2–4 | 2–3 times a week |
| Months 2–3 | weekly |
| Months 4–6 | every 2 weeks |
| Months 7–12 | monthly |
| After the first year | every 2–3 months |

Urine protein is checked once in the first month, every 3 months in the first year, and yearly after
that (recommendation 8.2). Your team also checks your anti-rejection medicine levels
([Prograf label][PROGRAF]).

The handbook's [sample transplant menu](../eat/menus/transplant.md) is built around about 56–70 g of
protein a day for a 70 kg adult and under 2,000 mg of sodium, with no potassium or phosphorus limit
unless your blood tests need one. Your dietitian sets your own numbers.

## What changes now

**Medicines, every day, on time.** Not taking anti-rejection medicines as prescribed is linked to a
high risk of rejection and of losing the kidney ([KDIGO 2009 transplant][K09TX], chapter 11). Use
alarms and a pill box, and never run out.

**Interactions.** Do not eat grapefruit or drink grapefruit juice with tacrolimus ([Prograf
label][PROGRAF]). Pomelos, Seville oranges and tangelos may act the same way ([FDA][FDAgrapefruit]).
St John's wort lowers tacrolimus levels and can lead to rejection ([Prograf label][PROGRAF]). Tell
every doctor, dentist and pharmacist that you have a transplant before you take anything new.

**Potassium and magnesium.** Tacrolimus can raise blood potassium and lower magnesium
([Prograf label][PROGRAF]). Your team may ask you to limit potassium or take magnesium; follow your
blood tests, not a fixed rule.

**Glucose.** Tacrolimus and steroids raise glucose, especially early on and after rejection treatment
([KDIGO 2009 transplant][K09TX], chapter 15; [Prograf label][PROGRAF]). Expect your insulin needs to
change; your team sets the plan. After a **successful pancreas-kidney transplant**, glucose can return
to normal and insulin may stop, but only your team decides that ([ADA 2026 §9][A26-9]). Keep checking
glucose until they tell you otherwise.

**Infections and vaccines.** Avoid live vaccines ([KDIGO 2009 transplant][K09TX], 12.2;
[Prograf label][PROGRAF]). Ask your team before any vaccine.

**Sun.** The risk of skin and lip cancer is very high after a transplant. Limit sun exposure for life,
wear protective clothing, and use a broad-spectrum, high-factor sunscreen
([KDIGO 2009 transplant][K09TX], 18.1–18.2; [Prograf label][PROGRAF]). Check your own skin and lips and
report new spots (18.3).

**Pregnancy.** Wait at least a year and plan it with your transplant team
([KDIGO 2009 transplant][K09TX], 25.2.1). **Mycophenolate** can cause miscarriage and birth defects.
It must be stopped or replaced **before** trying to get pregnant (25.2.2). Women who could get
pregnant need contraception counseling and must use acceptable birth control during treatment and for
6 weeks after stopping; men taking it, or their partners, are advised to use effective
contraception during treatment and for at least 90 days after ([CellCept label][CELLCEPT]). In July 2026 the FDA ended the mycophenolate REMS program, but these
label warnings still apply ([FDA][FDA-MYCO26]; [REMS][REMS]). This handbook does not give pregnancy
advice beyond this: see [Sex, fertility and pregnancy](../living/sex-fertility-pregnancy.md), which
refers you to specialists.

## Food safety

Anti-rejection medicines make food poisoning more dangerous. The USDA and FDA advise transplant
recipients ([USDA–FDA][FSTX]; [FoodSafety.gov][FOODSAFE]):

| Avoid | Choose instead |
|---|---|
| unpasteurized (raw) milk, and soft cheeses made from it (such as some feta, brie or queso fresco) | pasteurized milk; hard or processed cheeses; soft cheeses clearly labeled "made from pasteurized milk" |
| raw or undercooked meat, poultry, eggs and seafood (such as sashimi and some sushi) | food cooked to a safe temperature: ground beef 160 °F (71 °C), poultry 165 °F (74 °C), seafood 145 °F (63 °C) |
| hot dogs and deli meats straight from the pack | heated until steaming hot, or 165 °F (74 °C) |
| raw sprouts (alfalfa, bean and others) | cooked sprouts |
| refrigerated smoked fish | canned fish and seafood |

Use a food thermometer: the color of meat is not a reliable sign that it is safe ([USDA–FDA][FSTX]).

## What to do

- [ ] Take every anti-rejection dose on time; set phone alarms and use a weekly pill box.
- [ ] Never run out: order refills a week early, and plan for travel.
- [ ] Go to every blood test, even when you feel well.
- [ ] Remove grapefruit, pomelo and St John's wort from your kitchen and cupboard.
- [ ] Check with the transplant pharmacist before any new medicine, supplement or vaccine.
- [ ] Take your temperature if you feel unwell, and call the center if it is over 100 °F (37.8 °C).
- [ ] Use sunscreen and a hat every day you are outside, and book a yearly skin check.
- [ ] Follow the food safety table above; buy a food thermometer.
- [ ] Check your glucose as your team asks, and report highs and lows.
- [ ] Update your treatment to "transplant" in the app ([First setup](../app/first-setup.md)).

## Examples

**A missed dose.** You realize at lunch that you forgot your morning dose. Do not double up on your
own. Call the transplant center or the on-call pharmacist now and do what they say. Ask for a written
missed-dose plan for next time.

**A cold that will not go away.** It has been 10 days and you are still coughing. NIDDK lists "a cold
or cough that won't go away" as a reason to call your transplant center ([NIDDK][NIDDK-Tx]).

**A breakfast swap.** Grapefruit halves at the hotel buffet are out. Choose berries or an apple
instead.

## Ask your care team

1. What should I do if I miss or vomit a dose of an anti-rejection medicine?
2. How often will my blood tests be, and who tells me the results?
3. Do I need a potassium limit or a magnesium supplement?
4. How will my insulin plan change while I take steroids?
5. Which vaccines can I have, and when?
6. When is it safe to plan a pregnancy, and which medicines need changing first?

## Get help now if…

!!! danger "Call 911 (or your local emergency number)"
    Fever with shaking chills; chest pain; severe breathlessness; fainting; a low the person cannot
    treat themselves (give glucagon, then call) ([ADA 2026 §6][A26-6]); possible DKA: blood ketones of
    3.0 mmol/L or more, or any ketones with vomiting, deep fast breathing or fruity breath
    ([2024 consensus][HC24]). Full list: [Get help now](../get-help-now.md).

!!! warning "Call your transplant center right away"
    A fever over 100 °F (37.8 °C); drainage from your scar; burning when you pass urine; a cold or cough
    that will not go away; or you just do not feel well. Anti-rejection medicines can hide the signs
    of an infection ([NIDDK][NIDDK-Tx]). Also new swelling or a rise in blood pressure, which can mean
    the kidney is not clearing salt and fluid ([NIDDK][NIDDK-Tx]); much less urine
    ([KDIGO 2009 transplant][K09TX], 8.1); and vomiting or diarrhea that stops you keeping your
    medicines down, because missed doses risk rejection ([KDIGO 2009 transplant][K09TX], chapter 11).
